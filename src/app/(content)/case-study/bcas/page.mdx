## The problem

**A regulated entity and the regulator argue about compliance in rounds, and nothing bounds the argument.** No defined turnaround for a Security Programme audit. No limit on how many rounds a single observation can run. It takes months, and nobody can say how many.

I spent a day at Lucknow airport with an IPS officer, regional directors and architects, asking each of them the same question: what are you waiting on. Every friction had one shape. The entity prepares something, the regulator responds, the entity revises, it goes back. The loop was the cost, not any individual screen.

Inside the Security Programme module that loop is at its most formal, and its most expensive. The assessment runs to 106 questions, and every one of them can spawn its own thread of disagreement. Answering a single observation meant first reconstructing everything already said about that clause, and with observations scattered across 106 questions, nobody could see what was still outstanding.

## Three variants, one shipped

I built a sandbox to hold what the AI generated rather than accept the first output. Three variants of the observations workspace, evaluated against one question: can the entity see what is still on them?

**Variant A, notes pinned to each clause.** Zero context switching, the observation sits on the item it belongs to. But across 106 questions the open observations scatter and nothing answers *what is still on me*. It optimises for reading one and makes surveying all of them impossible.

**Variant B, assessment and review side by side.** The clearest read of an exchange. Also hungry for width, and awkward on the laptops reviewers actually use.

**Variant C, a docked observation workspace.** Every open observation in one place with a visible count of what is left. Its cost is real: it is a second place to be, so every rule reference has to be reproduced exactly right. Get that wrong in a regulatory product and you have built a liability.

I took that cost, on one condition, and the condition became the design.

> Instead of sending people back to the rule, I brought the rule to them.

## How four designers held one system

Five modules and every role across 18 entity categories is where a design team quietly starts producing separate products.

The system is role-based. One document model, one set of states, and permissions deciding what a role can see and do. A workspace is a view assembled from those permissions, not an interface someone hand-built. Change how an observation behaves and it arrives correctly for the airline's authorised signatory, the programme director who raised it, and the Director General approving the file.

That held while I was the only one building. It broke when we became four.

Output drifted. Hardcoded values crept in where tokens belonged. The same component grew three interpretations, and the thing became hard to maintain long before it became hard to design. Four people directing AI without shared constraints do not produce one product, they produce four.

So I built the context layer. An `/ai` folder holding who we design for, how the work flows and the domain it lives in, plus `DESIGN.md` for the system itself. Then `AGENTS.md`, the rules every session follows: what to check, what never to skip. I trained the three designers on it and reviewed their output against it.

That file is the difference between 487 screens that feel like one product and 487 that feel like four people in separate rooms.

## The call I got wrong

I built module one with Figma Make. Right tool for one module, and I would choose it again. What I got wrong was not re-examining it when the scope went to five.

Module one alone consumed **14,500 AI credits**: the 3,500 included with our organisation plan across two months, plus 7,500 bought outright. Five modules on that trajectory was not a budget anyone would approve, and I only did the arithmetic when we were already deep into module two.

So we migrated mid-flight, reverse-engineering everything in progress onto a Claude Code pipeline, building the context layer during the move rather than before it. It cost real time. It is also the only reason the guardrails exist.

## What happened

We won the pitch. Leadership had a real product to interact with rather than a deck describing one, and the agency's engineers valued the frontend build time it took off their plate. The tell was the timing: engineers joined **after** the win, and inherited frontend they could build on.

The honest part. eBCAS 2.0 is still in progress and I have no usage data yet. What was validated is the pitch, not the outcome. And there is no baseline to compare against, because the process I replaced had no defined turnaround and no bound on rounds. An unmeasured process is exactly why a legible one matters, which is why time-to-close per observation, split by which side is holding it, is the first thing I want instrumented.

Two things I would fix before that. The collapsed row tells you whose turn it is but not whether this is a one-line clarification or a four-round dispute. And an entity that believes an observation is wrong replies in the same box it would use to comply, when contesting a finding is a different act with a different outcome.
