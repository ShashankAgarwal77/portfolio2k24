## The hardest part was a conversation

The trickiest thing to design wasn't a screen — it was a back-and-forth. An airport submits its security plan; a senior regulator reads it, leaves notes on specific clauses, the airport responds, and it continues — with a clear record of every exchange — until the plan is approved or sent back. Two people, one document, real stakes on both sides. Making that conversation feel effortless *was* the design.

## Three directions, one that fit the reviewer

I tried three ways to hold that conversation before committing.

Notes-on-the-clause won because the reviewer thinks in *the document*, not a task list — and time at the airports confirmed it: reviewers wanted to stay in the clause they were reading. The queue's scanning strength came back later as a handy second view.

## An AI that speaks our design language

Four designers shipped 487 screens because the AI never guessed — it worked from our world, our system, and our rules. Three layers made that possible.

<NumberedPrinciples>
<Principle title="First, teach it our world">
An `/ai` folder holds everything the AI reads before it designs: who we design for and the jobs they're doing, how the work flows, and the domain it lives in — plus `PRODUCT.md` (what the product is) and `DESIGN.md` (our design system, written down). I set this up with the impeccable skill, so every session starts from the same shared understanding.
</Principle>

<Principle title="Then build the system before the screens">
From `DESIGN.md`, the AI builds the design system first — real components, real tokens. Only then does it generate screens on top, pulling context from the `/ai` folder. Screens inherit the system instead of reinventing it, so they stay consistent by default.
</Principle>

<Principle title="And keep it coherent with guardrails">
An `AGENTS.md` file sets the rules every session follows — what to check, what never to skip. It's what keeps 487 screens feeling like one product, and production-ready rather than drifting apart.
</Principle>
</NumberedPrinciples>

## Shipping production-ready, not a mockup

We won. Leadership's read was exactly what we'd aimed for: *this is closer to production than we expected at this stage.* The tell was the timing — engineers joined only **after** the win, and picked up frontend they could build on, not a mockup to redo.
